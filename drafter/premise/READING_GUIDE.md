# READING GUIDE — *Eight Tuesdays*

Authoritative on book-specific interpretation. Where this guide conflicts with generic defaults in `long_novel_task.md`, this guide wins. Where it is silent, the task prompt and the shared check system govern.

---

## 1. Form binding

- **POV / tense:** Close third person, past tense, **dual POV alternating by chapter** — June and Theo. Strict alternation may flex near Turn 3 (two consecutive June chapters during the rupture are permitted, once). June opens the book; June closes it.
- **Chapter shape:** ~2,000–3,500 words. The eight Tuesdays are the spine; most weeks get one class chapter plus one or two off-Tuesday chapters (cohort life, work life, the slow business of falling). Architecture decides the exact map.
- **Assignment cards (binding flourish):** Each of the eight class weeks opens with **Odile's handwritten assignment card** as a short epigraph — her wording, her voice, 10–40 words, occasionally with a crossed-out first draft of the instruction. These cards are load-bearing: on reread, they should read as a woman conducting two specific people toward each other.
- **No prologue, no epilogue labeled as such.** The showcase coda is the final chapter. If the agent wants a beyond-the-ending beat, it must live inside that chapter (e.g., the last assignment card, repurposed).
- **Length:** ~80K; band 75–88K. See premise.

## 2. Register & voice

- **Tone:** Warm, precise, lightly comic. Quiet interiority over quippiness. The comedy is observational and character-true — June's dryness, Theo's over-competent flailing, Odile's brutal economy — never sitcom rhythm, never banter for banter's sake.
- **Comp calibration (rhythm/texture only — never content, names, images, or sentences):**
  - Beth O'Leary — the warmth and the found-family social fabric;
  - Emily Henry **at about 60% banter density** — dialogue has spark but characters are allowed to answer questions sincerely;
  - *Days at the Morisaki Bookshop* / *Before the Coffee Gets Cold* — the quiet, the dignity of small routines, the unhurried scene that earns its smallness.
- **Voice differentiation (dual POV must be audible blind):** June's chapters: shorter sentences, exact visual nouns (a painter's eye — she names pigments, edges, kinds of light), self-interruption, jokes aimed at herself. Theo's chapters: longer, steadier syntax; logistical tenderness (he thinks in errands done for people); a habit of cataloguing what people need; his noticing is domestic where hers is optical. A reader should identify the POV within a paragraph with the names removed.
- **Odile on the page:** Economical. She gets the best lines and the fewest. Never whimsical-old-lady, never oracle. Her kindness arrives disguised as logistics or insult.
- **Interiority discipline:** Feelings are rendered through action, object, and dialogue first; named directly only when the naming *is* the event (e.g., the confession). No paragraph-length emotional weather reports.
- **Plain-speech floor (binding):** In every dialogue scene, each principal says at least one direct, unguarded sentence — a want, a fear, a fact, or a question stated plainly, not angled. Conversations may carry subtext but must be *followable as text*: after any exchange, a reader can say what each party asked, learned, or refused without decoding. Oblique or coded exchanges are a spice (at most ~2 per chapter), not the default register. Deflection must be visible *as* deflection, not dressed as wit. The strongest beats in this book are direct ones ("So if you quit, I lose the class." / "Yes."); protect that register.
- **No dialogue diagnosis (binding, zero tolerance):** Characters never comment on, characterize, or rate one another's language, phrasing, rhythm, or delivery — no "that had the rhythm of a partial answer," no calling a sentence careful, rehearsed, lawyerly, or load-bearing. Not once per chapter; not at all. Perceptiveness is shown by what a character *does* with what they heard, never by captioning the conversation. (Odile included: her diagnoses target the person or the work, never the sentence.)

## 3. Heat level

**Closed door / sweet.** Kisses on the page, written with full attention. Anything beyond fades at the scene break. Desire is rendered through specificity of attention (what the POV character notices, hands, paint on skin) rather than anatomy. No coyness about the fade — cut clean, no winking at the reader.

## 4. Motifs — owned set, with caps

Motifs recur; they must not be hammered. The mechanical gate's generic caps apply; these are the book-specific ones:

| Motif | Use | Cap |
|---|---|---|
| Hands (painting, carrying, fixing, almost-touching) | The book's primary tenderness channel | Foreground at most once per chapter; week-4 assignment is its sanctioned showcase |
| Light (November gold, gallery track lights, studio fluorescents) | June's painter-eye; the season clock | One deliberate light-image per chapter max; never "golden hour" |
| *Winter Kitchen* (Odile's painting) | June's origin and the book's thesis: domestic seen truly = love | Appears/invoked at most 5 times total; each appearance must add new meaning |
| The unfinished / the underpainting | Theo's arc; things begun and abandoned | Metaphorical use max once per act; literal use free |
| First snow | The clock made visible | Weather may be tracked throughout; snow itself falls once, at the end |
| Coffee / the diner booth | The cohort's and then the couple's commons | Scene-setting free; do not let ordering coffee become a tic |

Banned as motifs (too worn for this genre): rain at emotional peaks, shared umbrellas, paint "accidentally" on the face as flirtation more than once, the skyline as feelings.

## 5. Care moves (the book's love language — use, vary, don't announce)

Small acts are how this book says everything. Each named character owns distinct moves; never caption them ("it was these little things that..." is banned reflection).

- **Theo:** remembers exact orders/preferences after one exposure; fixes things wordlessly (a wobbling easel, a jammed window); arrives with the thing you mentioned needing two weeks ago; absorbs logistics so others can be present.
- **June:** names what's actually good in someone's work (precise, unsentimental praise — rare and therefore devastating); notices when someone's been left out of a conversation and re-threads them in; gives honest warnings dressed as jokes.
- **Odile:** teaches the person, not the room — assignments quietly tailored; insults that are diagnoses; feeds people without comment.
- **Cohort:** each couple gets at least one owned care move; design in architecture.

## 6. Tic catalog — refuse on sight (extends the shared pattern registry)

Genre-specific patterns this book must not contain, beyond the global BANNED/CAP tiers:

- "a breath she didn't know she'd been holding" and all variants; breath-catching generally capped hard
- stomach/chest "flips," "flutters," warmth "pooling," electricity at first touch
- "something shifted between them," "the air changed," unspecified "tension"
- smirking; chuckling; "a beat"; one-word reaction paragraphs ("Oh."— allowed at most once, at the confession, if earned)
- the love interest "didn't know he was beautiful" framing in either direction
- makeover logic, jealousy-via-third-party-date contrivance, eavesdropped half-conversation misunderstanding (the Turn-3 rupture is structural and earned; no cheap misunderstanding before it, and **no second rupture after the confession**)
- NYC postcard montage; "the city that never sleeps"; subway meet-cute clichés; "only in New York"
- art-world satire as cruelty (gentle comedy fine; contempt is off-register)
- epiphany-via-mirror; characters watching themselves feel things
- weather as on-the-nose emotional underline (see motif caps; the first-snow ending is the one sanctioned convergence)
- dialogue diagnosis in any form (see the zero-tolerance rule in §2) — any instance is a defect, in any speaker's mouth or in narration about speech
- **"load-bearing" / "loadbearing" / "load bearing" — banned outright**, all scopes, including literal structural use; mechanically enforced via `premise/patterns/reading_guide_bans.tsv`

## 7. Architecture questions Phase 2 must settle (open by design)

1. Exact chapter map across the eight weeks (class chapters vs. off-Tuesday chapters; where the POV alternation lands relative to the three turns).
2. The cohort: the three couples — names, textures, owned care moves, one small arc each, and which of them (if any) clocks the truth early.
3. The specifics of June's out-of-town offer — city, what makes it genuinely good, who offers it.
4. The discovery mechanism for Turn 3 — small, accidental, undeniable; not snooping, not a third party's reveal-monologue.
5. The joint piece: what June and Theo actually paint, such that it can carry the coda without a speech explaining it.
6. Whether the "couples only" rule predates opening night — and how a rereader could tell.
7. Bench vs. diner booth for the confession (or the walk between them).
8. What, if anything, the class learns at the showcase about either secret.

## 8. Book-specific quality gates (judgment, not regex)

- **The blind-POV test:** strip names from any page of interiority; if the POV isn't identifiable, the voice differentiation has failed.
- **The reread test:** every Odile scene must be consistent with her knowing; at least five moments planted that read innocently forward and pointedly backward — without any of them winking.
- **The smallness test:** each chapter must contain at least one specific, non-portable detail of place, craft (painting rendered with technical truth — gesso, sight-size, fat over lean), or cohort life. If a scene could be set in any city or any class, it isn't finished.
- **The warmth floor:** no scene's comedy lands at a character's expense in a way the book doesn't repair; embarrassment is survivable on the page within two scenes.
- **The earned-stay test:** June's choice to remain in New York must be legible as *for herself first* — if the ending reads as staying-for-a-boyfriend, it has failed the book's thesis.
- **The followability test:** take any dialogue exchange and ask a cold reader what each party wanted, learned, or refused. If the answer requires decoding subtext line by line, the scene has slipped into cleverness-as-register and fails the plain-speech floor (§2).
