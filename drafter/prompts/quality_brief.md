# Novel Quality Brief

This brief distills review criteria and craft priorities for a long autonomous novel-writing run. It is guidance, not a required workflow. Use it as a quality lens while designing whatever process best serves the book.

Workflow note: the autonomous run uses architecture phases (premise interpretation, architecture, voice cards, adversarial critique, chapter cards, drafting with cross-model synthesis, full-book critique, final assembly) plus available adversarial passes (Dialogue Doctor, Positive Prose Audit, Comedy Doctor when the premise is comic, Fresh-Reader Clarity Pass, Figurative Language Audit). Premise-specific calibration lives in the reading guide under `premise/`. Use this brief to sharpen the agent's craft judgment at any of those stages, not to replace per-chapter judgment with broad praise or abstract critique. If this brief conflicts with the reading guide for a specific book, the reading guide wins.

## Primary Aim

Write the strongest possible novel from the premise. The manuscript should feel complete, surprising, emotionally alive, structurally controlled, and excellent on the terms of its genre. Prefer choices that improve the reader's experience over choices that merely satisfy a checklist.

Aim for the most alive, inevitable, original version of this premise, capable of standing with the best books in its genre. The book should not feel like a remix of familiar comps, tropes, or recent successful novels. It should deliver the pleasures of its genre while producing images, situations, pressures, character contradictions, turns, and emotional consequences that feel newly discovered.

Reader enthusiasm is a craft outcome, not a compromise. The target reader should want to keep reading, recommend the book, revisit favorite scenes, and remember lines, images, turns, and characters because the story delivers momentum, attachment, surprise, payoff, and emotional force without sacrificing coherence, prose control, or originality.

## Novelty Within Genre

Novelty is a central requirement, not an optional flourish. The book must be highly creative without becoming incoherent, arbitrary, or novelty-for-novelty's-sake.

Before committing to major plot, world, character, or formal choices, ask:

- What is the obvious version of this genre move?
- What would a competent but derivative book do here?
- What have readers seen too many times?
- What specific new pressure, inversion, image, rule, relationship, or consequence does this book add?
- Does the choice deepen the premise, or is it merely decorative weirdness?
- Does the choice create story energy that later chapters can use?

Seek novelty in:

- premise execution, not just premise concept
- character motives and contradictions
- plot causality and reversals
- world rules and their human cost
- scene situations and social pressure
- dialogue rhythms and subtext
- images, objects, rituals, institutions, and recurring motifs
- the ending's emotional and conceptual turn

Avoid:

- generic genre furniture with renamed labels
- stock chosen-one, quest, rebellion, court intrigue, academy, dungeon, apocalypse, or romance beats unless transformed by the specific premise
- jokes or clever inversions that do not change consequence
- worldbuilding that is elaborate but dramatically inert
- novelty that breaks continuity, motivation, or reader trust

Review the manuscript for derivative drift. If a scene, subplot, character, or world element feels like it could have been imported from another book, revise it until it belongs uniquely to this one.

## Generative Invention

Avoiding cliches is not enough. The book needs positive invention: moments, objects, pressures, reversals, and behaviors that make the story feel discovered rather than assembled.

When planning or revising, generate:

- scene situations where the premise creates a problem no other book would create in quite the same way
- objects, places, institutions, rituals, games, jobs, meals, debts, injuries, habits, jokes, and social rules that carry story pressure
- character misbehavior that reveals desire, fear, pride, shame, or love without explaining it
- reversals that arise from character contradiction rather than author cleverness
- comic business or tonal texture that belongs to the characters' actual circumstances, not narrator commentary floating above them
- motifs and images that change meaning across the book
- consequences that make earlier choices feel irreversible

For major scenes, consider writing a quick "ordinary version" and then a "this book only" version. Prefer the latter unless the ordinary version is deliberately being used for contrast, rhythm, or realism.

## Genre And Premise Promise

Before drafting, define the book's specific promise:

- What experience does the premise offer that only this book can deliver?
- What genre pleasures must be honored?
- What familiar genre moves should be complicated, refreshed, or avoided?
- What emotional pressure should accumulate across the book?
- What should the ending make newly clear, painful, funny, satisfying, or irreversible?

Review every major decision against that promise.

## Whole-Book Review

At major milestones, read the manuscript as a complete book and ask:

- Does the opening create a live promise that the ending pays off?
- Does the book become more original as it develops, or does it retreat into familiar genre machinery?
- Does the protagonist or central pressure change in irreversible stages?
- Does each major section add pressure, knowledge, intimacy, danger, contradiction, or consequence?
- Does the book avoid middle sag, repetitive scene functions, and merely procedural movement?
- Does the ending feel earned by prior action rather than explained after the fact?
- Does the story's genre machinery remain legible without becoming mechanical?
- Does the prose sustain a distinctive stance without calling attention away from the scene?

Turn diagnosis into concrete revision tasks.

## Readability Under Complexity

Do not equate clarity with simplicity. A best-in-genre novel may be dense, strange, lyrical, comic, technical, procedural, romantic, horrific, or formally risky. The question is whether the reader is oriented enough to enjoy that difficulty on the book's own terms.

Check:

- whether scene action, physical orientation, focalizer perception, and plot-bearing consequence are available on first read
- whether new terms, names, rules, institutions, relationships, or formal patterns become plain enough before the chapter asks them to carry plot weight
- whether mysteries are deliberately atmospheric, suspenseful, funny, uncanny, romantic, horrific, or otherwise genre-apt, rather than accidental underexplanation
- whether the prose gives the reader plain footholds at moments of high conceptual, emotional, procedural, or action load
- whether aliases or near-synonyms make one concept look like several

Good clarity does not require glossary exposition. Prefer scene-native orientation: a task being performed, a body moving through space, a focalizer's recognition or misrecognition, a social consequence, a practical question, a cost, a change in leverage, or one plain clause that tells the reader what kind of thing a new term is.

## Cross-Chapter Audit

Track the book across chapter boundaries:

- continuity of time, place, physical state, relationships, injuries, objects, names, and world rules
- setup and payoff, including promises that need to return later
- unresolved threads that have gone quiet too long
- repeated emotional beats, repeated openings, repeated proof of baseline traits, and recurring explanatory patterns
- character motivation and knowledge state
- escalation: whether later chapters add new pressure rather than restating old pressure
- whether world rules or speculative mechanics stay consistent and are introduced before they carry plot weight

When a contradiction appears, decide whether to revise the earlier setup, the later payoff, or the underlying plan. Keep the source-of-truth files current.

## Local-Window Review

Review adjacent clusters of chapters for:

- transitions and chronology
- whether each chapter earns the next
- repeated scene shapes or openings
- pacing rhythm and chapter length feel
- emotional whiplash or emotional flatness
- dialogue voices staying distinct from scene to scene
- whether tension peaks are spaced well
- whether endings cut on live pressure instead of explaining the scene's meaning

Local review should catch problems that whole-book review can miss because they happen in the reader's immediate memory.

## Chapter And Scene Quality

A chapter should have a live local engine, not just serve the outline. A scene should change the available pressure, knowledge, relationship, risk, or desire. Watch for:

- scenes that could happen in many other books with only names changed
- scenes that explain instead of turning
- dialogue that summarizes a conflict instead of enacting it
- action without consequence
- cleverness that does not change character or pressure
- late-arriving motivation
- interiority that repeats what the action already made clear
- endings that continue after the chapter has landed

If a chapter is weak, consider a full rewrite rather than small repairs.

## Dialogue And Voice

Dialogue should sound arrived at in the moment, not prepared for the reader. Check:

- distinct character rhythm, diction, evasions, and habits
- subtext: what each speaker wants but does not say cleanly
- interruption, mishearing, deflection, pressure, and status movement
- whether the exchange changes the scene
- whether the line belongs to this character in this moment
- appropriate interiority around dialogue: enough thought, perception, silence, bodily reaction, and misinterpretation to make the exchange psychologically real, but not so much that the scene explains itself twice
- followability: after a consequential exchange, a first-time reader should be able to say what each speaker wanted, what shifted, and what the focalizer now understands or misunderstands

Be ruthless about fake-sharp dialogue. Cut or rewrite:

- exchanges that exist mainly to deliver a compact aphorism
- noun-question / portentous-answer patterns
- "in [abstraction]" lines that make the literal scene feel like a prop
- dialogue where every speaker has the same dry correction rhythm
- zingers that arrive at the moment a character should be confused, tired, afraid, ashamed, ordinary, or quiet
- lines that sound quotable but not speakable
- loaded short lines followed by unmarked silence when the silence carries tactical, emotional, or informational weight but the focalizer registers nothing about what it costs, who watched it, or what it changed

Claude can be especially useful for dialogue diagnosis, alternate versions, and scene rewrites, but final integration should preserve book-level continuity and taste.

## Prose

Favor concrete pressure, specific physical detail, and focalized perception over abstract explanation when the book's chosen register calls for it. Use figurative language when it reveals how a character perceives the world under pressure, not as default ornament. Aim for extremely high-quality prose: precise, alive, controlled, rhythmic, and specific to this book's consciousness — whatever register that consciousness operates in.

Compression is a tool, not a resting proof of style. A run of clipped, fragmentary, highly elliptical, or heavily image-driven sentences should eventually give the reader a grounding beat: what the focalizer wants, what the room or body is doing, what changed, or what action comes next. This is a rhythm judgment, not a sentence-count rule. The right amount depends on the book's genre, POV distance, pressure, and chosen register.

Figurative language should pass an earned-figuration test. Ask of every simile, metaphor, personification, aphoristic image, or heightened comparison:

- What literal fact, emotion, social pressure, danger, comedy, desire, or sensory event does this image clarify?
- Would this focalizer plausibly reach for this image, in this register, under this pressure?
- Does the image arise from this book's world, objects, labor, rituals, technology, weather, body, class texture, relationship history, or current scene materials?
- Does it sharpen the scene, or merely decorate intensity?
- If the nouns or setting could be swapped and the image would still work in another novel, should it be cut or rewritten?
- Would a sharp human reader admire it, accept it invisibly, or pause and think "come on"?

Prefer literal precision over a weak or portable image. Strong figuration should feel inevitable after the sentence lands; weak figuration makes the writer visible.

Cut or revise:

- abstract nouns doing emotional work that the scene could do
- balanced sentence patterns that sound composed rather than felt
- repeated gestures, expressions, and interior summaries
- paragraphs that restate the meaning of action the reader already understood
- generic menace metaphors, especially clean/knife/wound/shadow/cold formulations that sound intense but do not add new physical or psychological information
- figurative language that could describe any dangerous character in any book
- personification or metaphor whose only job is to make the prose sound literary, ominous, or profound

Before accepting a metaphor, ask what the focalizer literally sees, hears, smells, touches, fears, wants, or remembers. If the image does not sharpen that focalized perception, replace it with concrete observation.

## Revision Discipline

Reviews should produce bounded findings with acceptance tests. For each major finding, record:

- problem
- evidence
- likely cause
- revision direction
- acceptance test
- whether it was fixed, rejected, deferred, or transformed

Do not let review become endless commentary. Revise the manuscript.

Revision should continuously elevate the book. Each major pass should try to make the manuscript more original, more coherent, more emotionally exact, more dramatically pressured, more naturally voiced, and more beautiful at the prose level.

## Final Readiness

Before finalizing, confirm:

- the full manuscript is assembled in order
- no notes, TODOs, review comments, or planning text remain in the prose
- character names, world terms, relationships, and chronology are consistent
- central promises and major setups are paid off or deliberately left resonant
- the ending changes how the reader understands the book
- the final report honestly names remaining risks
