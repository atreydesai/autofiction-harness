## 1. Choosing an idea that can survive a novel (Academic manual, verbatim)

> A promising novel idea is not just "interesting." It must sustain **duration**, **causality**, and **variation**. Forster's distinction is useful here: many ideas can generate story ("and then, and then"), but fewer can generate plot, because plot requires cause-and-effect pressure. If your idea cannot answer "why does the next major event happen because of the previous one?" it may be a premise fragment, vignette, or short story seed rather than a novel engine.

> A durable selection workflow is to test each idea against five questions. First, **who wants what** that cannot be had easily? Second, **what force resists them**—person, institution, environment, self-division, or incompatible duty? Third, **why now**, rather than last month or next year? Fourth, **what escalates** if the character keeps acting? Fifth, **what changes irreversibly** by the end? If you cannot answer these cleanly, do not panic; the answer may emerge through exploratory writing. But if after several passes the idea still yields only mood, backstory, or worldbuilding without forward pressure, it is warning you that the premise lacks engine.

> A technically strong premise statement is best written as a **dynamic sentence**, not a topic label. Compare "a novel about grief in a coastal town" with "after a forensic accountant discovers that her brother's death was staged as an accident, she must decide whether to expose the town's most powerful family before the same cover-up consumes her own career." The second premise contains agency, conflict, stakes, and causal movement. It is not "better" because it is more commercial; it is better because it already implies scenes, reversals, and decisions. That is the real criterion.

> A useful exercise here is the **premise ladder**. Freewrite for ten to fifteen minutes on an idea. Then reduce that freewrite into one sentence, then one sentence with a clear causal hinge, then one sentence naming the protagonist's dilemma, then one sentence naming the irreversible choice at or near the climax. If each reduction makes the idea sharper rather than thinner, you probably have a viable novel. If each reduction kills the project, you probably do not yet know the real story.

## 2. From idea to premise (Novel process, verbatim)

> A raw novel idea is usually too weak to carry a book. "A wizard school," "an isolated marriage," or "a city where memory can be traded" are settings or premises-in-seed form, not usable narrative engines. A working novel premise normally contains at least five elements: a focal character, a destabilizing problem, a desire or task, meaningful opposition, and consequences.

> A reliable premise template looks like this:

| Element | Diagnostic question | Example prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Protagonist | Who suffers most if the story fails? | "A newly promoted homicide detective…" |
| Destabilizer | What breaks normal life? | "…discovers the supposed suicide was staged…" |
| Objective | What must be achieved? | "…and must identify the killer before…" |
| Opposition | Who or what blocks success? | "…a politically protected suspect and her own department…" |
| Stakes | What is lost if she fails? | "…bury the case and frame her partner." |

> That template is not formula for its own sake. It forces you to convert abstraction into narrative pressure.

## 3. Concept distillation (verbatim)

> Write one sentence that captures the novel's central engine. Ingermanson's Snowflake Method starts exactly here: a one-sentence summary, ideally short enough to function as a ten-second selling tool. If you cannot state the story in a sentence, you usually do not yet understand what the book is principally about.

> This stage is where many weak projects should be killed or merged. It is cheaper to discard a beautiful but inert premise now than after 80,000 unusable words.

## 5. Expansion into a story paragraph (verbatim)

> Snowflake's next step—expanding the one-sentence premise into a paragraph with setup, major disasters, and ending—is a powerful diagnostic move because it forces macro-shape into view early. Ingermanson's own model uses three large disasters plus an ending, mapping naturally onto end-of-Act-One, midpoint, end-of-Act-Two, and resolution logic.
